# BOMA-SHIELD
Drought pushes wildlife out of core conservation areas and into neighbouring farms and settlements in search of water and forage. Around ecosystems like Amboseli, this triggers a dual crisis: destroyed crops, killed livestock, and — in response — retaliatory killings of the very wildlife conservancies exist to protect. Current early-warning tools treat drought monitoring and wildlife conflict as separate problems, so no system tells a household or a ranger team where and when an incursion is likely before it happens.
Boma Shield closes that gap. It generates a weekly, zone-level incursion-risk score for the grazing corridors and settlements bordering a conservancy, combining satellite-derived drought signals with how close farms sit to the park boundary and water points, and with the conservancy's own historical conflict incident records. Instead of a general seasonal warning, Boma Shield tells a ranger team "this specific corridor is high-risk this week" and tells households in that corridor to reinforce their boma or move livestock — giving them days of lead time to act rather than reacting after an incursion.
Primary users are agropastoral households living along the conservancy boundary and the ranger teams who patrol it. Secondary beneficiaries are conservancy management, who gain a data-driven tool for deploying limited patrol capacity, and the wildlife populations that benefit when fewer incursions escalate into retaliatory killings. The prototype targets a single conservancy landscape as a proof of concept, with a clear path to scaling the same pipeline to other conservancy boundaries across the IGAD region.

Boma Shield builds a per-zone risk score using three inputs. First, vegetation and moisture stress: NDVI and soil-moisture anomalies pulled from Sentinel-2/MODIS via Google Earth Engine, combined with CHIRPS rainfall data, to quantify how dry conditions are inside and outside the park relative to normal. Second, spatial exposure: distance from each farm/settlement cluster to the park boundary and to mapped water points, using OpenStreetMap data. Third, history: past human-wildlife conflict incidents (location, date, species involved), sourced from conservancy or KWS records where available, or approximated from published incident studies for the prototype if raw logs aren't accessible in time.
These three inputs feed a lightweight classifier (gradient boosting or random forest via scikit-learn) trained to associate combinations of dryness and proximity with historical incursion timing. The model outputs a weekly risk level — low, medium, high — for each pre-defined boundary zone, rather than one conservancy-wide number.
The key innovation is specificity and actionability: the output isn't a raw drought index but a named corridor with a concrete suggested action ("Zone 3, high risk this week — reinforce boma / move livestock to guarded corridor"). Rangers see all zones on a simple web dashboard; registered households in a flagged zone receive the same alert by SMS via Africa's Talking, so no smartphone or data connection is required to act on it.
Key technologies: Python, Google Earth Engine, CHIRPS/ERA5, OpenStreetMap (Overpass API), scikit-learn, PostgreSQL, a Streamlit or React dashboard, Africa's Talking SMS API.
